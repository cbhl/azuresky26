I'd say the one takeaway from this book appears on page 100: "Add routines and rituals to your working life designed to minimize the amount of your limited willpower necessary to transition into and maintain a state of unbroken concentration." This one sentence, I am convinced, is true.<br/><br/>But the examples of routines given are not great. Most are an appeal to celebrity: Donald Knuth requiring snail mail over email, Jerry Seinfield keeping track of streaks on a calendar on his wall, J.K. Rowling checking into a five-star hotel to finish the last of the Harry Potter books, quit social media, 37signals' four-day work weeks ("summer fridays"). In terms of practical advice to apply to my own life, I liked Make Time by Knapp and Zeratsky better.<br/><br/>I'd also skip the entire first part, the first 90 pages or so. The need to focus has arguably become only more self-evident in the zoom/chromebook/paperless/discord/slack generation, and so those chapters have not aged so well since the book was written back in 2016. Schools experimenting with going phone-free and going back to dead-tree paper schedules and worksheets is evidence of this, as is the popularity of apps like Hank Green's Focus Friend.
